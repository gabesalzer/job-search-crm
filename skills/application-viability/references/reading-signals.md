# Reading the material

What actually predicts advancement, and what only sounds like it does. Work
through this while reading the transcript or thread; quote what's found.

## The central distinction

Almost everything said in an interview is either **cheap** or **costly**.

Cheap signals cost the speaker nothing and are said to nearly every candidate:
praise, enthusiasm, "great questions," "we'll be in touch." Their base rate is
so high that hearing them tells you almost nothing about which candidate is
being advanced.

Costly signals commit the speaker to something — their time, their calendar,
their colleagues' time, information they'd have to walk back, or a position
in front of other people. Those are the ones that discriminate.

When weighing any line, ask: would they have said this to a candidate they
were about to reject? If yes, it's noise.

## Interview transcripts: signals that carry weight

**Specificity about the user's future.** Not "you'd do well here" but naming
the actual first project, the person they'd sit next to, the problem waiting
for them. Concrete futures are hard to improvise for someone you're rejecting.

**Selling.** The moment the interviewer starts persuading — pitching the team,
pre-empting objections about comp or stage or roadmap, asking what else is in
the user's process — the frame has flipped from evaluation to recruitment.
This is among the strongest signals available.

**Unprompted logistics.** Volunteering next steps, timelines, who else the
user will meet, start dates. Especially when specific and unrequested.

**Objection handling.** Raising a concern about the user's fit *and then
arguing themselves out of it*, or asking follow-ups clearly aimed at getting
comfortable rather than at deciding. Someone building a case is on their side.

**Time given.** Running long by a meaningful margin, or pulling in another
person mid-call. Calendar time is genuinely scarce.

**Internal detail.** Sharing something they wouldn't tell a stranger — a
reorg, a struggling team, why the last person left, real budget constraints.
That's a trust move.

## Interview transcripts: signals that carry weight downward

**Depth that never arrives.** The conversation stays at surface level
throughout, the interviewer doesn't probe, questions come off a list. Little
effort spent is usually little interest.

**Ending early.** Especially with time left and no attempt to use it.

**Unaddressed objections.** A concern raised, the user answers, and the
interviewer moves on without engaging. Frequently means the concern stood.

**Vague futures.** Every forward-looking answer hedges: "if things move
forward," "the team would decide," "we're still figuring out the process."
Contrast with specificity above.

**Requirements the user visibly doesn't meet, restated.** Coming back to the
same gap more than once is the interviewer rehearsing their reason.

**Process ambiguity introduced late.** Mentioning other candidates, a paused
req, a hiring committee that "sometimes takes a while," when earlier
conversation was cleaner. Expectation-setting for a no, or for a long wait.

## The polite noise list

These read as positive and are close to informationally empty on their own:

"Great questions." · "This was a great conversation." · "We'll be in touch."
· "You have a really interesting background." · "I'll pass this along to the
team." · "Feel free to reach out with any questions." · "We're moving quickly"
(said without a date) · "I really enjoyed talking with you."

They are not *negative* — they're just the floor. Treat their absence as
mildly informative and their presence as nearly uninformative. Never build an
upward adjustment on these alone.

## Email threads: different physics

Tone matters less in email. Structure matters more.

**Latency, in both directions.** How fast does the other side reply, and is
it getting faster or slower across the thread? A recruiter who replied in two
hours and now takes six days has told you something. Compare against their own
earlier pace, not an absolute standard.

**Who is driving.** Count who initiates. A thread where the user sends three
messages for every one back is a thread that's ending, whatever the words say.

**Reply length trajectory.** Shortening replies across a thread is one of the
more reliable decay signals in email.

**Specificity of commitment.** "Early next week" versus "Tuesday at 2." Named
dates are commitments; vague windows are placeholders.

**Who gets added.** Looping in a hiring manager, a scheduler, a compensation
contact — each is a costly internal action.

**Broken commitments.** A stated timeline that passed with no message is
strong negative evidence, and it's the single thing most often read as neutral
because nothing happened. Nothing happening *is* the datum.

**Channel shifts.** Moving to a call, or to a personal address, usually means
acceleration. Moving from a named person to a no-reply system address usually
means the opposite.

## Weighing the sender

Read every signal against who is producing it. A recruiter's enthusiasm is
partly their job; a hiring manager's is their actual opinion. An agency
recruiter is selling a placement and their warmth is weakly correlated with
any specific employer's intent — the app tracks agencies separately for
exactly this reason. Signals from someone with decision authority outweigh the
same words from someone without it.

## Source shapes the prior

The application's Source is real information about the pursuit's starting
position:

- **Referral** — arrives with borrowed credibility and usually a faster,
  more responsive process. Adjust up modestly at early stages. But check
  whether the referrer has actual pull; a warm intro from someone with no
  standing behaves like an outbound application with better manners.
- **Recruiter Inbound** — the employer initiated, which is a real signal of
  interest in the profile. Discount when the outreach is visibly templated or
  the role is a poor match for the user's background: that's volume sourcing,
  not interest.
- **Outbound** — the default competitive case. No adjustment; the base rates
  are largely built from these.

## When the material is thin

Assess what's there and say plainly what's missing. A twelve-line scheduling
thread does not support a confident number. Widen the uncertainty, name the
one or two things that would sharpen it, and recommend leaving the score blank
rather than inventing precision — blank and low are different claims.
